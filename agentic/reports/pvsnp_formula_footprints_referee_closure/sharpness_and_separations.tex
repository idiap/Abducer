\subsection{Sharpness status and model separations}
\label{subsec:sharpness-status}

The lower bounds above should be read as necessary conditions until matching
families are supplied.  Two elementary examples clarify the model boundaries.

First, formula labels and occurrence labels can differ by a factor \(M_n\).
Let \(M_n\) be odd and let every row contain \(M_n\) copies of a single generated
key \(u_x\), with \(x\mapsto u_x\) injective.  In the formula-label model the
row output under parity is the shared value \(\lambda(u_x)\), so one generated
formula controls the row.  In the occurrence-label model the \(M_n\) positions
are private, and forcing a parity row requires all \(M_n\) occurrences.  Thus
the occurrence model has the full aggregator-forcing cost that the formula
model can avoid through equality of generated strings.

Second, equality quotienting can collapse private occurrence hardness.  If each
row contains two equal occurrences and parity is the aggregator, then quotienting
the two positions identifies their labels and the row contribution is
\(\lambda(u)+\lambda(u)=0\).  The quotient carrier is constant, although the
private occurrence model before quotienting could realize arbitrary row
parities.  This is why the quotient theorem is a distinct equality-aware
completion statement rather than a cosmetic rewriting of the occurrence model.

These examples do not provide a matching upper-bound theory for explicit hard
SAT-labeled carriers.  In the absence of such a theory, the row-forcing and
quotient-distance results are supporting structural lemmas.  The formula
footprint theorem remains the main technical candidate because the batched
static dictionary improves the naive \(M K\ell\) exception-lookup cost to a
near-linear batch-plus-dictionary cost.
